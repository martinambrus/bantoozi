City council approves riverside park after two-year debate

By Maya Lindqvist · September 25, 2026

The Riverton city council voted 7 to 2 on Wednesday night to turn the disused rail yard on the east bank of the river into a public park, ending a debate that has divided the city for more than two years. The decision clears the way for construction to begin next spring, with the first section expected to open to visitors in the summer of 2028.

The eleven-hectare site has been fenced off since the freight line closed in 2019. Developers had proposed several housing projects for the land, but residents of the surrounding neighbourhoods argued that the east bank already lacked green space and that the riverside should remain open to everyone. More than four thousand people signed a petition asking the council to keep the land in public hands.

"This is the largest new park the city has created in half a century," said council member Ruth Okafor, who introduced the proposal. "Children who grow up on the east bank will be able to walk to the river instead of looking at it through a fence." She added that the plan had changed considerably since it was first presented, after more than a dozen public meetings.

The approved design includes a wetland area that will absorb floodwater during heavy rain, a network of walking and cycling paths, two playgrounds and a small amphitheatre built from the old loading platforms. Several of the brick warehouses along the tracks will be restored and leased to cafés, workshops and a community boatyard. The city's engineers say the wetland alone should reduce flooding on nearby streets by a third.

Not everyone is convinced. The two council members who voted against the plan warned that its estimated cost of 38 million euros could rise once the soil, contaminated by decades of diesel and coal, is fully tested. "We are promising a park without knowing what is underneath it," said council member Tomas Berg. He also questioned whether the city could afford the maintenance of such a large area when existing parks are already short of staff.

Supporters of the plan point to last month's budget vote, which set aside a reserve for environmental clean-up, and to a regional grant that is expected to cover almost a quarter of the construction costs. The city has also agreed to review the budget every six months and to publish the results of the soil tests as soon as they are available.

Local businesses along Mill Street, which borders the site, have largely welcomed the decision. The owner of a bakery that has operated there since 1987 said she hoped the park would bring more people to a street that has struggled since the rail yard closed. Others are worried about rising rents once the area becomes more attractive, and a tenants' association has asked the council to protect long-term residents.

Construction will take place in three phases. The first will clean up the soil and build the wetland, the second will add the paths and playgrounds, and the third will restore the warehouses. The city expects the whole park to be finished by 2030, although officials acknowledged that the timetable depends on the results of the environmental survey that begins next month.

The council will hold a public meeting on October 14 to present the detailed schedule and to answer questions from residents. Anyone who cannot attend in person will be able to follow the meeting online and submit questions in advance through the city's website.
