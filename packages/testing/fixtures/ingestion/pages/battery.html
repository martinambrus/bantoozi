Solid-state batteries move from the lab to the pilot line

By Jana Novak

Three European manufacturers announced this week that their solid-state battery cells have left the laboratory and entered pilot production. The cells replace the flammable liquid electrolyte of today's lithium-ion batteries with a thin ceramic layer, which the companies say allows a denser anode and faster charging without the risk of thermal runaway.

Engineers at the pilot plant explained that the hardest problem was not the chemistry itself but the manufacturing tolerances. The ceramic separator has to be pressed against the electrodes with a uniform force across the whole cell, otherwise tiny gaps grow into dendrites after a few hundred charging cycles. The new line uses heated rollers and an inspection camera that rejects every sheet with a visible defect.

Independent researchers welcomed the announcement but warned that pilot volumes are small. A car needs several thousand cells, and the first customers will be makers of premium vehicles that can absorb the higher price. Mass-market models are not expected before the end of the decade, when recycling of the new materials should also be solved.

The companies plan to publish cycle-life data from the first production batch next spring, together with the results of crash and nail-penetration tests carried out by an independent laboratory.
